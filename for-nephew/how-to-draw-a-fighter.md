# How to draw a fighter

You draw it. We put it in the game. It walks, it jumps, it punches people off the screen.

Here is how to draw one so that it works. These rules are not about making your drawing *good* —
draw whatever you want, it is your character. They are about making it possible for the computer
to cut your drawing into pieces and move the pieces around. A great drawing that breaks these
rules is harder to get into the game than an okay drawing that follows them.

There is a nicer version of this page, with pictures and a real example, at
<https://claude.ai/artifact/DmdTZS491FFJs28Cj8g5qL> — send that one. Keep the two in step if you
change either.

---

## Before you start: it has to be built like a person

A head, a body, two arms, two legs. It does not have to *be* a person — a robot, a wizard, a
shark with legs, a knight made out of toasters all work perfectly. But it has to be put together
like one, because the game moves your character by swinging its arms and legs.

A car, a floating eyeball or a blob has nowhere to put the legs, so it cannot fight yet. Draw
those anyway if you want them — keep them for later, and see the last line of this page.

---

## The four rules that matter most

**1. Outline in dark marker or pen. Not pencil.**

The computer finds your character by looking for dark lines on white paper. Pencil is too faint
and too grey — it gets confused with the paper itself. Use a black marker, a Sharpie, or a
ballpoint pen and press hard. You can colour it in with pencil crayon afterwards, that part is
fine. It is the **outline** that has to be dark.

**2. Draw big. Fill the whole page.**

Bigger drawing means more detail survives when it gets into the game. A character drawn in the
corner of the page comes out blurry. Take up the whole sheet.

Drawing it big does not make your character big *in the game* — we set that separately, from
your answers on the character sheet. Draw big so the detail survives, not to make a giant.

**3. Draw it from the SIDE, facing RIGHT.**

Not from the front, not from behind. From the side, like your character is walking past you and
you took a photo. Head in profile — you see one eye, and the nose sticks out.

```
      GOOD                      NOT THIS

       (o>                         (oo)
      /|                          \ |  /
     / | \                         \|_/
       |  \                        / \
      / \                         /   \
     /   \

  side view,                   facing straight
  facing right                 at you
```

**Facing right matters.** Always right, every character, every time. In the game it turns around
on its own — but it has to start out facing right or things end up backwards.

Side view is harder to draw than front view and there is a real reason we are asking for it. In
a fighting game, the most important thing you need to see instantly is **which way somebody is
pointing**, because that is how you know if they are about to hit you. From the front you cannot
tell. From the side you always can.

**4. Spread the arms and legs out so nothing touches anything.**

This is the weird rule, and it is the one that breaks drawings most often. We have to cut your
character into pieces — head, body, arms, legs — and we can only do that if we can see where one
piece ends and the next begins.

The easy way to do this in side view is to draw your character **mid-stride, like they are taking
a big step**:

- one leg forward, one leg back, a clear gap between them
- one arm forward, one arm back, both held away from the body
- a bit of a gap at the neck
- **a real bend at every elbow and every knee** — do not draw the limbs as straight sticks

Do not let an arm lie flat against the body, and do not let the legs touch each other.

The bends matter more than they look. Every arm and leg has to fold in the middle to walk and
punch, so we cut each one into two pieces. If you drew it as one straight line there is nothing
to tell us where the elbow goes and we have to guess — and a guessed elbow is what makes a
character move like it is broken.

**Once it is in the game the arms and legs move on their own**, so the pose you draw is not the
pose it gets stuck in. You are drawing the *parts*, not the pose. Draw whatever pose separates
the pieces best.

---

## The rest

**Plain white paper.** No lines, no grid, no graph paper. Printer paper is perfect.

**Close all your lines.** If you are going to colour it in, make sure the outline has no gaps
for the colour to leak out of.

**Do not smudge or shade with the side of your pencil.** Smudges look like dirt to the computer
and it tries to cut them out as if they were part of your character.

**Both arms and both legs, all four visible.** From the side, a real person would have one arm
hidden behind the other. Draw it anyway — swing the far arm back so you can see all of it. Same
with the far leg. We need all four pieces.

If you really cannot make all four work, draw the near arm and near leg clearly and we will
build the far ones from them. One good arm beats two mushed-together ones.

**A head that is clearly separate from the body** — draw a neck, even a short one, or just leave
a little gap. If the head is jammed straight onto the shoulders we cannot tell where to cut.

**Weapons and held things are great.** Sword, hammer, wand, whatever. Draw it held out away from
the body so we can cut it out separately — then it can swing.

## Second page: draw your moves

On a **separate sheet**, draw the effects your character makes. Big. One per page if you can.

If your guy shoots fire, draw the fireball on its own — just the fireball, nothing else, as big
as your hand. If he summons lightning, draw the lightning bolt. If he punches so hard the air
explodes, draw the explosion.

This is the fun part and it is the part that makes your character feel like *yours* in the game.
The fighting moves everybody has are kind of the same. The fireball is what makes people go
"whoa, what is that".

**Want it animated?** Draw the same effect two or three times in a row, slightly different each
time — a small fireball, a medium one, a big one. We play them in order and it moves.

## Taking the photo

This part is easy to get wrong and it wastes a good drawing, so:

- **Bright, even light.** Near a window in the daytime is ideal. Overhead room light is fine.
- **Hold the camera straight above the paper**, not at an angle. The paper should look like a
  rectangle, not a trapezoid.
- **Watch for your own shadow.** If your head or the phone is blocking the light and casting a
  shadow across the page, move.
- **Get close enough that the drawing fills the picture**, with just a little white paper
  showing around the edges.
- **Flat paper.** No curl, no fold, no bend. Put a book on it for a minute first if you need to.

Scanning it instead of photographing it is even better, if there is a scanner around.

---

## One last thing

Fill out a **character sheet** for each fighter — that is the other page. It asks what your
character is, what it is great at, what it is terrible at, and what its coolest move is. Those
answers are what we use to decide how it plays.

The "what is it **terrible** at" question is not a trick. Every good fighter in every game is
bad at something, and that is what makes fighting them interesting. A character that is the best
at everything is boring and nobody picks it.
